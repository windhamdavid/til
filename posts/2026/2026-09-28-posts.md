Today I learned that a hearing aid can cause feedback on a large symphony hall sound system. 

<!-- truncate -->

We went to see a performance of Mozart's Requiem[^1] by the Greenville Symphony Orchestra for their opening weekend and because it fell on our 22nd anniversary. The foreshadowing happened when I texted this photograph to a group thread saying 'spot the old people' before the symphony took the stage. 

![](/img/requiem_hearing.jpg)

And let me first say that I make that joke a good bit because we've found that the old people are usually right. The go-to-bed-early, matinee-shows, and read-the-Sunday-paper sorta lifestyle started suiting us before age 50. 

Given the running time of _Requiem_ sits at under an hour, the performance began with some contemporary pieces. During the second one which featured some abstract moments of silence we started noticing a bit of feedback[^2] from the audio system. Ginny asked to confirm that it wasn't just her hearing it. 

And just as the main act was to begin and the chorus was seated on stage, the conductor came out to ask that everyone in the audience please take a look at their hearing aid settings and adjust appropriately. 

![](/img/requiem_hearing-2.jpg)

I thought to myself, how can a multi-million dollar facility with, what I would guess to be, a stellar audio system possibly be affected by someone's little hearing aid stuck in their ear. But afterwards, we did the responsible internet expert thing to figure it out and sure nuff - it happens. 

The cause is an electromagnetic induction loop. The Peace Center uses a specialized telecoil system that allows patrons to pipe audio directly to their compatible hearing aids. If a person is within proximity of an unshielded mic line or pickup, it can create an induction loop. And what we noticed is that it was particularly sensitive to the flute. The dynamic range compression featured in modern hearing aids strains on the quiet delicate flute solos and then crank it up to compensate. 

A flute produces a very pure fundamental sine-wave frequency that when sustained can mimic audio feedback because the audio systems in both the system and the hearing aid use automatic gain control to crank up the amplification. The audio engineer should 'ring out the room' by artificially pushing feedback into an empty hall so they can notch out problem pitches. And since this room is host to the same symphony on the regular, I couldn't peg it on them. There is a possibility that the magnetic field from the loop bled back into the system. The hearing loop systems are often wired around the perimeter and under the railings of balconies. Modern hearing aids use wide dynamic range compression to amplify really soft sounds and any area wrapped in a copper active loop wire could pick it back up. The giveaway was the 'warble' in which modern hearing aids try to aggressively quell the feedback by shifting the pitch which could be heard as the trailing pitch changing over the audio system. 

The feedback became apparent in the second opening piece, which was _Sound and Fury_ by Anna Clyne[^3] which features a monologue from _Macbeth_[^4]:

>Life's but a walking shadow, a poor player,  
That struts and frets his hour upon the stage,  
And then is heard no more. It is a tale  
Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury,  
Signifying nothing.

I noticed that a fella on our balcony had a hearing aid because of the volume of his voice while he was expressing his displeasure with the first arrangements to his wife. I joked that it was him causing the problems and we've now discovered that much like a Sunday buffet, there are some drawbacks to following the old folks. However, the juxtaposition of the monologue next to the _Requiem_ alongside the hearing aid debacle mixed just perfectly with the overarching theme which I later described with just three words.  

> **Lord Have Mercy**
