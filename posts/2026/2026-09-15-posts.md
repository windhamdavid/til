Today I learned what it looks like when a denial of service arrives at **one request per second** and takes the whole box down anyway.

<!-- truncate -->

Woke up to a wall of alert mail about [Woozie](/docs/computers/woozie)[^1], the box that serves everything here. From outside it pinged fine, SSH answered, port 80 answered — and 443 was closed, every HTTPS site timing out. Then it'd come back for a minute or two and go again. That intermittency turned out to be the most misleading part of the whole morning, and I'll come back to it.

The thing that finally explained it was the Apache scoreboard, which I only had because I'd fixed `/server-status` the day before for an unrelated reason:

```sh
BusyWorkers: 76
IdleWorkers: 1
```

Seventy-six workers busy, one free, out of a pool of 150. Meanwhile the actual traffic was **572 requests in ten minutes** — about one a second. Nothing. I've served more than that sitting still.

That's the whole attack in two numbers. The workers weren't busy *doing* anything. They were holding.

## Slowloris

The name is a real animal[^2], and it's apt — the thing is slow on purpose. You open a connection to a web server and start sending an HTTP request, then simply never finish it. Dribble out a header every so often, just before the timeout would fire, and the server politely keeps waiting. It has to. It can't know whether you're an attacker or somebody on terrible hotel wifi.

Do that a couple hundred times and a server that runs **one process per connection** has no processes left. No flood, no bandwidth, nothing that looks like an attack in any graph you're watching.

My `Timeout` was the stock **300** — five minutes. So a single stalled connection could own a worker process for five minutes. At 150 workers, anyone willing to open 150 sockets and type slowly owns the entire server.

## Why none of my defences saw it

This is the part worth writing down, because I had a fair amount pointed at the wrong thing.

**The bot blocker matches user-agents.** This pool sends ordinary browser strings. Straight through.

**Blocking by IP reaches nothing.** There were about a hundred connections and **essentially one per address** — US mobile, Nigeria, Brazil, Russia, Italy, the UK. There's no offender to block. A blocklist is built on the assumption that an attacker is somewhere; this one is everywhere, once each.

**`mod_reqtimeout` was already enabled**, which fooled me for a bit. It wasn't missing. It was set to the stock `header=20-40` — a defence tuned for polite clients on bad connections, which is a different thing from a defence against someone being slow on purpose.

**Apache never crashed.** systemd showed it active the entire time. There was simply no free worker to answer, which from outside is indistinguishable from down.

**It wasn't memory.** 2.2GB genuinely used of 7.8GB. `ps` RSS sums to about 4.2GB, which had me looking in the wrong place until I remembered RSS counts shared library pages once per process.

Every single one of those is a defence built for **volume**. The attack had no volume.

## What bought time

Three things, in increasing order of how much they mattered — though none of them was the actual answer, which I didn't find until later.

**1. Shorten the window.** `Timeout` from 300 down to 60, and `RequestReadTimeout header=10-20` — ten seconds to send your headers, twenty at the outside. A real browser does it in milliseconds. Halving the header window is the single most effective knob there is against slow headers.

I put these in a **drop-in** rather than editing `apache2.conf`, because that file is a dpkg conffile and editing it means a merge prompt on every upgrade — the same prompt that nearly reverted my SSH hardening on [Zeke](/docs/computers/zeke)[^3] last week. The ordering is what makes the drop-in work: `apache2.conf` sets `Timeout` at line 92 and loads the modules at 147, but `conf-enabled/` gets included at **222**. Last one wins.

**2. More workers — which is not a fix.** I doubled the pool because at 150 against 219 held connections it couldn't answer anyone at all. But raising it just hands a connection-holder more processes to fill. It buys room to work, not a solution. Worth saying because it's the first thing everyone reaches for.

The sizing was measured rather than guessed: real marginal cost came out around **10MB per process**, not the 33MB `ps` implies.

**3. Remove the reason they were there.** They were walking deep repository paths on my [Gogs](/docs/host/Gogs)[^4] instance — every commit, every raw blob, each one triggering per-commit avatar lookups. Expensive pages, and they ignored `robots.txt`.

Turning on **required sign-in for viewing** meant anonymous requests got a cheap redirect to the login page instead of an expensive repo walk. Connections fell from **219 to 87 within minutes**. That's the one that actually ended it. The cost is that anonymous `git clone` over HTTPS stopped working, which on a single-user instance I can live with.

## The thing that actually ended it

I spent most of the day on the mechanism — connections held open — and every mitigation worked *a little*, which is precisely what kept me away from the cause. The question I wasn't asking was why a trivial request rate could exhaust the box **at all**.

The answer was a module that wasn't doing anything.

`mod_php8.4` was loaded. It also served nothing — every vhost was already handing PHP to `php8.3-fpm`, for the same ordering reason as the drop-in above: `conf-enabled` loads after `mods-enabled`, so its handler wins. The module had exactly one remaining effect on the system, and it's the one that mattered: **mod_php forces the prefork MPM.** And prefork is one process per connection.

So the module was dead weight that happened to be pinning the entire server into the worst possible shape for this attack. Disable it, switch to the event MPM, same traffic, same attacker, minutes apart:

| | prefork | event |
|---|---|---|
| BusyWorkers | **300** — pinned at the ceiling | **16** |
| IdleWorkers | 0 | 34 |
| apache processes | 301 | **3** |
| apache memory | **6,592 MB** | **132 MB** |
| sites | all timing out | all 200 |

Six and a half gigabytes down to a hundred and thirty. HTTP/2 also started working that same minute — `mod_http2` had been loaded and inert the entire time, warning on every start, because it can't run under prefork either. I'd been getting that warning for however long and never read it.

Remove a module that was doing nothing, and the attack stopped mattering.

## Three ways I misread my own config

All the same shape, which is what makes it worth writing down: **I trusted a command without checking its output meant anything.**

`grep -r` over `sites-enabled/` returns nothing, because it's all symlinks. I have this written down in my own notes for another box and walked into it anyway.

`apache2ctl -M` came back mangled, and I read that as "no mod_php loaded". **A broken result is not a negative result.**

A per-file grep matched a vhost, and I called it the PHP 7.4 site — but a vhost file holds more than one block, and the match was in the redirect-only one, which sends everything to https before PHP is ever reached. **Grep the block, not the file.**

And the one that nearly had me migrate 23 sites for no reason: the FPM configs are guarded by `<IfModule !mod_php8.c>`, which reads as "only when mod_php is absent" — so I declared them inert. The module registers as `php_module` from **`mod_php.c`**, not `mod_php8.c`. The guard never matches. The config was always active, and had been all along.

What settled it was asking the running process instead of the config file: `phpinfo()` reporting 8.3.33, and socket connections climbing under concurrent requests. **Configuration is a claim. The running process is the evidence.** When they disagree, it's the config you're reading wrong.

## Two things I'd want to know next time

**A graceful restart is useless here.** `systemctl restart apache2` just hung. "Graceful" means waiting for current connections to finish — and the held connections *are* the problem, so you're asking it to wait for the attack to politely conclude. `systemctl kill --signal=SIGKILL` and then start is what actually cycles it.

**A monitor restarting a service every ten minutes is a symptom, not a repair.** Mine had been restarting Apache since 06:52, each one buying one to four minutes before saturation. That's what produced the intermittent recovery that confused me at the start, and it kept the site nominally alive while hiding that the cause came straight back. The useful question isn't whether the restart worked — it's **how soon after a restart the failure returns**. A minute means the restart is treating a symptom.

There was a second, funnier problem sitting underneath: the monitor's health check was itself broken, and had been restarting a perfectly healthy Apache on a schedule for who knows how long. It requested `localhost` on port 80, no vhost answers to that name, so it fell through to the default vhost — which correctly returns **403**. The monitor read 403 as failure. During the incident that was disguised, because the failures read as connection errors while workers were exhausted and only switched to "403" once Apache recovered. It now checks `/server-status` from loopback, which has the advantage of proving request *processing* rather than just a listening socket.

## The real lesson

**Read the scoreboard, not the request count.** A wall of workers in `R` with zero idle and a trivial request rate is slow-connection holding, full stop. `server-status?auto` gives you busy, idle and the scoreboard in one call.

And the structural one: **one process per connection is the wrong shape for a public box.** That's what makes this cheap to do and expensive to absorb — the attacker spends a socket, I spend a whole process. The alternative parks idle connections without holding a process at all, which is most of why the [next box](/docs/computers/zeke)[^3] is going up on nginx.

But the bit I keep turning over is that I was in that shape by accident. Nobody chose prefork. It was imposed by a module that had quietly stopped doing its job at some point and stayed loaded, and I'd have gone on not knowing until something came along that could tell the difference. **Dead configuration isn't inert — it's just waiting for the conditions that make it matter.**

Cheapest attack I've ever been on the wrong end of. A few hundred sockets and some patience.
