Today I learned that explaining or coaching on AI to someone who doesn't write software is fundamentally about organization and it's utilization absolutely depends on it.

For the past while I've been building out a system for using AI that I named [Ralph](/docs/ai/ralph)[^2], after the Simpsons character, on the theory that you shouldn't name a thing you're relying on something that makes it sound cleverer than it is. Along those same lines, my stock trading app is named Gil, after Gil Gunderson[^3], the desperate one who can never close a deal. It's a sandbox and a parent server — it holds an index of my own notes and documents, a set of tools an assistant is allowed to use, and the written rules for how it should behave. The whole idea is that it gets reused rather than rebuilt — one supervisor I can point at any project I'm working on, personal or professional, instead of standing something up from scratch every time and re-explaining myself to it. The point of it is that the useful part of AI, for me, has almost nothing to do with the model and almost everything to do with what I've given it to work from.

I've spent a fair bit of this year both coaching and explaining AI to folks, in my personal life and my professional one. This has been mostly with people who aren't technical. What they tend to have in common is that their actual work product is documents — reports, policies, research, the endless accumulation of things that have to be written, referenced, and written about again. The pace of communications via email, meetings, and reports is absurd and these are jobs that run on paperwork and meetings. 

And it isn't only work, which is the part that seems to surprise people most. Some of the same friends who tell me AI is dumb will still open twenty tabs hunting for a recipe, scroll past somebody's childhood memoir on each one, and end up cooking the third thing they found. You can just ask. Gemini is already sitting on the phone in their pocket — it'll give you something matched to what's actually in your kitchen, and you can do the whole thing by voice with your hands covered in flour instead of poking at a screen. That isn't a technical achievement. It's knowing the thing is there and what it's good for.

I keep finding myself saying, over and over, in different words, is: put it in plain text and put it in a folder you control. That's the same thing I said in 2022.[^4] The argument then was portability, searchability, and usability — that the tool didn't much matter as long as your knowledge was organized, hypertext'd, indexable, and yours. I stand by all of it, but I had the reason slightly wrong. I thought plain markdown files in system folders were the right answer because software comes and goes and you don't want your notes trapped in something that gets acquired. That's still true. It just turned out not to be the important part.

The important part is that four years later, plain markdown in a folder is the format you can hand to an AI assistant.

## Garbage in, garbage out

Charles Babbage[^5] got asked twice, about his calculating engine, *"if you put into the machine wrong figures, will the right answers come out?"* — and wrote that he couldn't apprehend the confusion of ideas that would provoke the question. Computing later shortened this to *garbage in, garbage out*. It just means the machine can't tell good input from bad. It doesn't check. Feed it the wrong thing and you get a wrong answer delivered with all the confidence of a right one.

The wrinkle now is that garbage has a second meaning. It used to mean wrong. But a document can be perfectly correct and still be garbage to an assistant because it can't read it — a scanned PDF, a screenshot of a table, a file in some format nothing opens anymore. Correct and useless aren't mutually exclusive. Most people only worry about the first kind.

Everyone I've talked to about this has the same underlying problem and it isn't a technology problem. Documents pile up. They land in Google Drive, in email attachments, in a Downloads folder, on a shared network drive somebody set up in 2014. Getting organized is most people's whole workflow — it's the thing they mean when they say they need to get on top of their work. And it's genuinely harder now than it used to be, because things arrive faster and from more directions than any filing habit was designed for.

Until recently, "organized" meant *you* could find it. That was the whole test. Now there's a second test, which is whether something else can read it, and those two tests have different answers. A Google Doc is findable. It is not especially readable — not by you in ten years, not by a program, not by anything that isn't Google. Same for a Word file, a PDF of a scanned page, a screenshot of a table someone pasted into Slack.

What worries me is the direction this is heading. Adoption is not the problem — near as I can tell everybody is already using it, at work and at home, whether or not anyone decided they would. What I keep seeing is people pointing it at the pile exactly as it sits, and the pile isn't a thing you can point at. So you get an answer drawn from four versions of a document and no indication of which one it read.

And it doesn't only read. It writes, faster than any of us do, which means it also *produces* documents — summaries, drafts, notes about notes — that land right back in the same undifferentiated heap they came from. Then next month it reads those too, alongside the originals, with nothing marking which is which.

I don't think AI is going to clean any of this up. My honest worry is that it becomes another stratum of the mess, laid on top of the network drive from 2014 and the Downloads folder and the four handbooks — one more layer of confusion, arriving faster than the last one. Everyone I've watched get real value out of it sorted out what they were feeding it first. Nobody has skipped that step and come out ahead. We'll just end up doing stupid shit faster while making it more complex.

## The part nobody mentions

Here's what I didn't anticipate, and what I think is actually important. Most of what gets written about AI and personal knowledge management is about *retrieval* — feed it your notes so it can answer questions about them. That's real and it works. But it's the smaller half.

The bigger half is that the same plain files are how you give the thing instructions. Not prompts typed into a box that vanish when you close the tab. Files. Sitting in the folder. A page of plain sentences that says how you want reports formatted, what the acronyms in your field mean, which of the eleven versions of the handbook is the real one, what you never want it to do. The assistant reads that the same way it reads your notes, because to it they're the same thing.

I want to be precise about why this matters to someone who isn't technical, because I think it's the thing that flips it from a toy to a tool. Guardrails are the difference between an assistant that's occasionally impressive and one you can rely on. And writing guardrails in markdown requires exactly no programming. It's writing sentences down in a file — the skill everyone reading this already has and uses all day.

That's what I've been trying to get across. The markdown isn't there because it's a nice format for notes. It's there because it's the interface. It's the one place where storage, instructions, and the record of what you decided are all the same kind of object, in your hands, on your disk.

## What it actually gets you

I should say plainly what the payoff is, because I've noticed that the pitch for this stuff tends to float somewhere above the ground and people are right to be suspicious of it.

None of what's useful here is impressive. It's the boring work, done faster, by something that has actually read your files:

- **"What did we decide about this?"** — asked across four years of meeting notes, and answered with the note it came from, so you can go check. This is the one that converts people. Everyone has lost a decision.
- **The recurring report.** The one due every month in the same format with different numbers. Your notes have the numbers, a markdown file has the format, and the first draft stops being the part you dread.
- **"Does this draft have everything it's supposed to?"** — checked against the requirements, which are sitting in a file you wrote, because you're the one who knows what they are.
- **Which version is real.** You have eleven of them. It can tell you what changed between two, which is faster than reading both.
- **Turning a meeting into a list.** Notes in, action items out, in the shape you asked for.

It's the same handful in most fields with different nouns — the reading pile instead of the policy binder, the course material instead of the compliance report, the case file instead of either.

Nothing on that list is a new capability. Every one of them is something you could do yourself with enough hours. That's the point, and it's why I think the practical version of this is more interesting than the ambitious version. It's not going to do your job. It's going to do the forty minutes of retrieval and reformatting that stand between you and doing your job, and it'll do them against your material rather than something it half-remembers from the internet.

The unglamorous test I'd apply: does it save you an hour this week on something you were going to do anyway? If not, the setup isn't finished, or it isn't working. From there it's easy enough to imagine the end-to-end version — an assistant that reads your schedule and your reports and takes direction from you while you're driving. 

## Whatever works for you

People want a recommendation and I understand the impulse, but this is where I'd repeat what I said in 2022: whatever works for you is what's best. I use Obsidian and I use VS Code. Both open a folder of markdown files, which is the actual system — the app is just a window onto it. That's the test I'd apply to anything: if you uninstalled it tomorrow, would your work still be sitting there in a form you could read? If yes, the choice is reversible and you should stop agonizing over it. If no, it isn't a second brain, it's a rental. The folder is the product. The app is a preference.

The same question comes up about the assistant itself — which one, and which one for what. Same answer, and it matters less than people think. I run some models locally on my own machine and some remotely over the internet, and I move between them depending on what I'm doing. The local ones handle anything I'd rather not send off my machine and most of the routine work; they cost nothing to run and they work with the wifi off. The remote ones are better at the genuinely hard problems and I use them when the work warrants it.

The point isn't the split, it's that switching costs me nothing. The notes don't change. The instructions I wrote don't change. The folder has no idea which one read it last, and doesn't care. Models are interchangeable in a way the marketing around them works hard to obscure — they're improving fast enough that whichever one is best today won't be in a year, and if your material is portable you get to take the upgrade for free instead of migrating to it. That's the 2022 argument again, one layer up. Back then I was worried about your notes being trapped in an app. The same trap is open for models, and it's the more expensive one, because a company that owns both your documents and the only assistant that can read them has you twice.

What isn't a preference is what you put in it. In the last of these[^6] I said vibe coding is bullshite unless you actually know what you're doing, and the non-coder version of that isn't "learn to program." It's: know what's in your own files. An assistant working from a knowledge base you assembled carelessly will be confidently wrong in the shape of your carelessness — it'll cite the superseded handbook because you kept the superseded handbook. The competence that makes this work isn't technical. It's editorial. Knowing what's current, what's authoritative, what should have been thrown out three years ago. That's a skill most people whose work runs on documents have in abundance, and one most software people, myself included, have to work at.

Which is a more optimistic conclusion than I usually land on. The thing that makes this work well is the thing these folks are already good at. The part they think is the hard bit — the technology — is just a folder and a set of text files. I'm going to write the actual instructions up properly as a separate section rather than bury them in an essay, since the whole point is that it should be followable by someone who isn't me. I've started that at [AI Assistant](/docs/assistant)[^7], which is an outline more than a guide at the moment and will fill in as we work through it together.
